East Africa safari or Southern Africa safari?

East Africa is the classic safari many people picture. Southern Africa often feels more intimate, with several completely different ways to safari.

From Irene's original Africa Friday post — Aug 14, 2026

East Africa — especially Kenya and Tanzania — is the classic safari many people picture: endless plains, enormous herds, the Great Migration and tented camps under huge African skies.

Southern Africa — South Africa, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia and Namibia — often feels more intimate, especially in private reserves and concessions. Think smaller lodges, fewer vehicles and more flexibility in how guides track wildlife.

It also offers many different ways to go on safari. You can explore by car on game drives or join guided walking safaris in top regions such as Zambia. You can also take a boat or mokoro in water-rich areas like the Okavango Delta in Botswana. In some regions, night drives are also allowed so you can continue spotting wildlife after dark.

Some South African reserves are also malaria-free.

Want sweeping landscapes, enormous herds and the Great Migration? Look at East Africa.

Want a more intimate experience and several completely different ways to safari? Look at Southern Africa.

Neither is better. The right answer depends on the reserve, lodge, guide — and season.

How to choose between them

Start with what you most want to see. If the Migration and open-plains herds are the priority, East Africa is the natural first choice. If a quieter, more private feel and a mix of land and water matter more, Southern Africa is often the better fit.

Think about season. Both regions have a best time that depends on what you want to see, not just on the calendar. Migration timing, water levels, and dry-season game viewing all shift by month and by area.

Think about pace. East Africa trips often cover more ground between parks. Southern Africa trips can combine a reserve, a river, and a waterfall region in a way that feels less rushed, especially for a first safari.

Two practical differences that often tip the decision: in East Africa, national parks generally do not allow night drives or off-road driving, while many Southern private reserves and concessions do — which is why a Southern sighting can feel closer and more flexible. And in the Okavango, water levels are often highest in the dry season, when floodwater arrives from upstream, so a water-based trip there does not follow the usual wet-season logic.

There is no wrong first choice. Many travelers who start in one region return for the other — and the second trip usually feels completely different from the first.
