Traveling to Mexico with children? Bring their birth certificates.

If you're traveling to Mexico with kids, pack one more thing — and don't leave it to check-in to find out you needed it.

As of November 14, 2026, something changed for families going to Mexico, and I don't want any of my clients caught off guard by it at the front desk after a long travel day. If you're traveling to Mexico with kids — a family vacation in Cancun, the Riviera Maya, Puerto Vallarta, or Los Cabos — this Mexico new law for children affects your hotel check-in, not just your flight.

Hotels and other tourism providers in Mexico now have to verify, before they provide the service, that the adults traveling with a child are that child's parent, legal guardian, or custodian. Not just take your word for it — ask for official documents that prove it. This is Mexico's new proof-of-guardianship rule for minors at hotel check-in.

What changed: Mexico's new law for traveling with children

The change adds two new sections, X Bis and X Ter, to Article 58 of Mexico's General Tourism Law, the article that lists what tourism providers have to do. It went through the Chamber of Deputies, then the Senate approved it unanimously on October 7, the President signed the decree on November 10, it was published in Mexico's Diario Oficial de la Federación on November 13, and it took effect the next day, November 14, 2026.

It is a federal law, so it applies across Mexico, not just in Cancun or the Riviera Maya — also in Puerto Vallarta, Los Cabos, Playa del Carmen, and everywhere else families travel in Mexico. And it puts the responsibility on the provider, not on the traveler at the border. The hotel, the resort, the vacation rental, the tour company, the transfer, the restaurant or spa — they are the ones who have to ask, and they have to ask before the service starts, whether you booked in person or online.

What they have to establish is one of four things: parentage, parental authority, legal guardianship, or legal custody. In some cases they may also have to check that a young-looking companion is actually 18 or older, with official documents.

If they can't establish it, the law says they must refuse the service — deny service — and notify the investigating authorities about a possible offense. That's the part that makes this more than a polite question at check-in. A hotel can't just wave it through because you look like a family.

Why this is happening: protecting minors in tourism

The point of the law is to protect children and adolescents from child trafficking and sexual exploitation in tourism. Quintana Roo, where Cancun and the Riviera Maya are, is one of the most visited places in the world, and that is exactly why the reform was pushed from there. These are Mexico's new documentation requirements for minors traveling with adults.

I support that goal completely. I also know what it feels like to arrive tired, with kids in tow, and be asked for a document you left at home because nobody told you to bring it. So let's get the practical part right.

What isn't settled yet: no official document list for Mexico travel with minors

There is no official checklist yet, and no detailed protocols published yet for hotels to follow. Until that guidance comes out, what one hotel asks for may not be exactly what the next one asks for.

That includes the questions families always ask me: original or copy? Certified copy or a photo on your phone? Does it need to be translated into Spanish? Does a short-form birth certificate count, or does it need to be the long form that lists both parents?

I don't have a firm answer to those yet, because the authorities haven't given one. Don't take a Facebook post — including mine — as the final word for your specific hotel. Ask your travel agent to contact the hotel, in writing, before you go.

What to bring to Mexico with kids: birth certificates, passports, and proof of guardianship

Until there is an official list, this is what I recommend, and what I'm telling my own families to do for Mexico travel with children:

If both parents are traveling with their own children: each child's passport, plus a birth certificate for each child that shows the parents' names. The long-form or full copy, not a short abstract that leaves the parents off.

If only one parent is traveling: the same, plus a notarized consent letter — a travel consent letter for minors — from the other parent if you can get one, and something that explains a different last name if that applies — a marriage certificate, or a name-change document. It is a precaution, not a guarantee, but it is the kind of paper that helps a front desk say yes quickly.

If you're traveling with a grandchild, a niece or nephew, or a friend's child: the child's birth certificate, plus a notarized consent letter from the parents, and any custody or guardianship papers if they apply. If you are a stepparent traveling with a child who isn't your legal dependent, bring the same kind of proof of the parents' permission.

If you are a legal guardian or custodian: bring the court-issued guardianship or custody papers, not just a birth certificate. A birth certificate shows parentage — it doesn't by itself prove custody or guardianship.

If you're traveling with a teenager: don't assume a 16- or 17-year-old won't be asked. The law covers anyone under 18, and a provider may also ask you to prove a companion is 18 or older. Pack the same documents for your teenager as you would for a younger child.

A practical tip from years of doing this: keep a paper copy in your carry-on, separate from the originals, and a photo on your phone as a backup. Don't put the only copy in a checked bag, and don't leave it in the hotel safe on a day you're going on a tour or a day pass — the tour operator can ask, too.

Cruises, day passes, and tours in Mexico with children

This doesn't just affect a hotel check-in. Shore excursions and resort day passes are tourism services too, so a local operator can ask for the same proof of parentage or guardianship. Walking off a cruise ship at port is a different thing, but once you're booking a tour or checking into a day-pass resort, bring the papers with you in your day bag.

Hotel Xcaret Mexico: birth certificates at check-in

From our experience, Hotel Xcaret has been very strict about this, so if you are traveling there, make sure to have a birth certificate. They were already asking families for children's birth certificates at check-in before this new law took effect, and travelers have been caught out by it — not told in advance, then scrambling at the desk. With the new law in place, I would not go to Xcaret without one for each child, along with their passport.
